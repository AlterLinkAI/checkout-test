The Merrow Tide Guide

The Harbor of Port Merrow is a made-up place used only for tests. Its small fleet of boats depends on the Merrow Tide Guide, a booklet that the harbor office writes by hand every winter. The guide explains, in plain words, why the water in the harbor rises and falls twice a day and how the keepers plan their work around it.

The first rule of the guide is that the sea level in Port Merrow follows the Silver Moon, a made-up moon that circles the planet once every 24 hours and 50 minutes. Because of this, the high water comes about fifty minutes later each day. The keepers write the new times on a slate by the pier every morning, and the fishermen check the slate before they load their nets.

The second rule is the pull of the Amber Sun. The guide says the Sun pulls on the water too, but with less than half of the strength of the Silver Moon, because it is much farther away. When the Silver Moon and the Amber Sun stand in one line with the planet, their pulls add up and the harbor sees the Great Tides, which rise higher and fall lower than usual. When the two stand at a right angle, the pulls partly cancel and the harbor sees the Small Tides, when the water hardly moves.

The third rule is about the shape of the harbor. The guide explains that the long narrow bay of Port Merrow squeezes the incoming water, so the high water in the harbor is about twice as high as in the open sea. The keepers call this the Funnel Rule. On the other side of the cape, where the coast is wide and open, the same tide is small, and nobody there bothers to keep a slate.

The guide also lists the work of the harbor in order. At the first hour of the rising water the ferry boat leaves the old pier, because the channel is deep enough only when the water is high. At the middle of the falling water the keepers close the lock gate so that the boats inside the inner basin stay afloat. At the lowest water the children of the village walk out on the sand to collect shells, and the keepers ring the bell twice to call them back before the water returns.

Safety has its own chapter. The guide warns that the water comes back faster than a person walks, especially during the Great Tides, and that nobody should walk past the red marker poles when the bell rings. It also says that the slate is a plan, not a promise: a strong wind from the north can push the water higher by half a meter, and a long calm can leave it lower than the slate says. For this reason the keepers always add a safety margin of one hour to every crossing.

The last chapter of the guide is a short story of how the booklet began. Long ago, the first keeper of Port Merrow was a retired baker who noticed that his bread dough rose and fell with the same rhythm as the water. He started to write down the times of the high water next to his baking times, and after one year he had a table. His neighbors copied the table, and the copies grew into the guide that the harbor office still writes by hand every winter.

To sum up, the Merrow Tide Guide says that the water rises and falls twice a day because of the pull of the Silver Moon and, less strongly, of the Amber Sun; that the strongest tides come when they stand in one line; that the shape of the bay makes the tide higher; and that every plan on the pier has to keep a safety margin.
