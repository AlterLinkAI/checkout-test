How the tides work on the Fernhill coast

The sea at Fernhill rises and falls twice a day. The rise is called the flood tide and the fall is called the ebb tide. The two moments when the water is highest and lowest are called high water and low water, and about six hours pass between one and the next.

The main cause is the pull of the Moon. The Moon pulls on the ocean facing it, and the water piles up into a gentle bulge. A second bulge forms on the opposite side of the Earth, so most coasts see two high waters in each lunar day, which lasts about twenty-four hours and fifty minutes. This is why the tide table moves later by nearly an hour every day.

The Sun also pulls on the sea, but with less force because it is far away. When the Sun, the Moon and the Earth stand in a line at new moon and full moon, the two pulls add up and the tides are at their largest, called spring tides. At the quarter moons the pulls work against each other and the tides are small, called neap tides.

The shape of the coast changes the size of the tide. In the narrow Fernhill harbour the water is squeezed and the range reaches four metres, while on the open beach it is only about one metre. Strong wind and low air pressure can raise the sea above the table by several centimetres, which the keeper of the Fernhill Lighthouse notes in his logbook every evening.

Fishermen at Fernhill plan their trips by the tide table: boats leave the harbour on the ebb and return on the flood, and nobody crosses the sand flats in the last two hours before high water.
